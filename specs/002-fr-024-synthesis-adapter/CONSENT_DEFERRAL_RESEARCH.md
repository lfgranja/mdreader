# Research Findings — Deferred Consent for v1 (Clarification Batch C3 / SC-044 / Principle XI)

- **Slug**: fr-024-synthesis-adapter / C3
- **Created**: 2026-10-06 (subagent research for the parent session's C3 decision)
- **Question under research**: Should v1 defer cloud TTS and consent acquisition (relying on the verified on-device engine + `test_double` for the SC-044 demonstration), avoiding per-session consent prompts?
- **Scope note**: This document supplies the external evidence base. It does not override internal governance: `specs/002-fr-024-synthesis-adapter/spec.md` (batch confirmation 2026-10-06, C3-A **split**) already records the decision that consent acquisition is deferred and SC-044 stays `DEFERRED` (`test_double` inadmissible once a real adapter is registered, per `001/spec.md:89` and `002/spec.md:89`). The evidence below supports the consent-deferral half unconditionally and informs any future revisit of the SC-044 half.

## Executive summary

**Recommendation: defer cloud TTS and the consent-acquisition surface to post-v1. Acquire consent, when it lands, once per provider, outside the driving context. Never prompt per session.** Five independent evidence streams converge:

1. **Consent fatigue is empirically established** — repeated prompts train habituated acceptance that does not reflect user preference, which is precisely the consent GDPR says is *not* valid (Section A).
2. **No regulator requires per-session consent** — GDPR consent is purpose-bound and revocable (Art. 7(3)), and the EDPB itself requires electronic consent requests to be "not unnecessarily disruptive" (Recital 32; Guidelines 05/2020 §78). CCPA/CPRA uses notice + opt-out, not per-session opt-in (Section B/C).
3. **On-device synthesis collects no user voice** — the biometric trigger (GDPR Art. 4(14)/Art. 9; CPRA §1798.140(c)/(ae)(2)) is *processing voice to uniquely identify*. This app only *outputs* provider voice models; it never records the user. Art. 9 is not triggered by the v1 path at all (Section B).
4. **Driving-safety guidance forbids required visual-manual interaction while driving** — NHTSA's glance budget (≤2.0 s mean glance, ≤12 s cumulative) and Android's Car quality criteria (DD-2, DR-1 ≤2 s response, VI-1 "look only when safe") make a mid-drive consent prompt a design violation, not a UX nit (Section D).
5. **Offline-first is the privacy-protective default** — GDPR Art. 25 + Art. 5(1)(c), EDPB's endorsement of edge/local processing, and Android's fully-restricted "moving" state all favor zero-egress synthesis as the default, with consent gating only the enhancement (Section E).

## A. Consent fatigue (scientific literature)

- Cookie-consent research is the closest controlled literature to per-session prompting. **Bielova et al., USENIX Security 2024** (3,947 French participants): a "highlighted decline" banner doubled refusal (17% → 34%, p=0.000); a "consequences" banner tripled it (→ 47%); and **50% of users who accepted said they did so out of habit**; effects persisted across repeated exposures (habit formation). — [source: usenixsecurity24-bielova.pdf, §5.2, §5.3] (confidence: high)
- **Utz et al., CHI 2019** ("(Un)informed Consent", >80,000 unique users): "seemingly small implementation decisions can substantially impact whether and how people interact with consent notices"; nudging has a large effect; 48% of users believed clicking "decline" on a neutral banner would break the site. — [source: dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3319535.3354212] (confidence: high)
- **"Behavior with Privacy Banners at Scale", IEEE TMA 2023** (4M+ interactions, 400+ sites, global CMP): **only 1–4% of visitors opt out when more than one click is required**; a single-click "Reject All" raises denial to ~21% (~5×). — [source: iris.polito.it TMA 2023 paper] (confidence: high)
- **Nouwens et al., CHI 2020** ("Dark Patterns after the GDPR", n=680 scraped designs + 40-participant field experiment): removing the reject button from the first page increases consent by 22–23 percentage points; only 11.8% of designs met minimal legal requirements. — [source: dspace.mit.edu/...3313831.3376321] (confidence: high)
- **"Okay, whatever": An Evaluation of Cookie Consent Interfaces, CHI 2022** defines *privacy fatigue* as "the tendency of consumers to disclose greater information over time when using more complex and less-usable privacy controls"; absence of in-line options led users to consent to all cookies and reduced investment in the decision; fewer than half selected their stated preferred option. — [source: dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3491102.3501985] (confidence: high)
- **"The fatigued consent" (2023, master's thesis, think-aloud + interviews)**: "consent fatigue is the most decisive factor in a user's choice of cookies"; the large number of daily requests leads users to stop caring; extensive notice text induces fatigue. — [source: exa.ai/library/publication/szvw4r0j462] (confidence: medium — smaller qualitative sample)
- **Legal implication (the decisive point)**: GDPR Art. 4(11) consent must be *freely given, specific, informed and unambiguous*. EDPB Guidelines 05/2020 (§78): an electronic consent request "should not be unnecessarily disruptive to the use of the service"; Recital 32 repeats the requirement. A per-session prompt in a commute produces exactly the habituated dismissal the literature documents — consent that is *demonstrated* (a stored record) but not *freely given* (a deliberate choice). One deliberate, parked-context, per-provider decision is both safer and legally stronger than per-session prompting. — [source: GDPR Art. 4(11), Recital 32; EDPB Guidelines 05/2020] (confidence: high)

## B. GDPR Art. 7–9 and voice-as-biometric

- **Art. 7** (conditions for consent): (1) controller must *demonstrate* consent; (2) request in plain, distinguishable language; **(3) withdrawal at any time, as easy as giving**; (4) service must not be conditioned on consent to processing not necessary for the service. — [source: Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Art. 7] (confidence: high)
- **Art. 9(1)–(2)**: processing of biometric data "for the purpose of uniquely identifying a natural person" is prohibited unless an exception applies; (2)(a) is *explicit* consent. **Art. 4(14)** defines biometric data as data "resulting from specific technical processing relating to the physical, physiological or behavioural characteristics... which allow or confirm the unique identification"; **Recital 51** states images are not *systematically* biometric — only when processed through technical means allowing unique identification. The threshold is the *identification purpose*, not the modality. — [source: GDPR Art. 4(14), 9, Recital 51; gdprcommentary.com Art. 9 commentary; ICO special-category guidance] (confidence: high)
- **EDPB Guidelines 02/2021 on Virtual Voice Assistants** (adopted 7 July 2021): distinguishes four VVA purposes and bases — executing user requests (**no prior consent needed**), ML improvement (consent is the only appropriate basis), **biometric identification (explicit consent, Art. 9)**, profiling. The guidelines note voice data "is inherently biometric personal data" (§2.1) — the stricter reading — but the operative test remains Art. 4(14)'s unique-identification threshold (see EBF consultation response, 23 Apr 2021: "consent is only required when the voice is used for identification purposes"). The guidelines also explicitly endorse **edge computing/local processing** as a privacy-favorable paradigm. — [source: edpb.europa.eu Guidelines 02/2021 v2.0; EBF response edpb_consultation...ebf_045080] (confidence: high)
- **Nautsch et al., "The GDPR & Speech Data", Interspeech 2019**: biometric processing = speaker recognition/verification use; incidental speech processing for other purposes does not per se trigger Art. 9. — [source: isca-archive.org/interspeech_2019/nautsch19c_interspeech.pdf] (confidence: high)
- **Application to this product (key finding)**: the synthesis path *generates* speech from text using provider voice models (`faber`/`cadu`/`jeff`, or cloud voices). **The device never captures, records, or uploads the user's voice.** Therefore: (i) no biometric data *of the user* is processed → **Art. 9 is not triggered by on-device or cloud synthesis**; (ii) the only personal-data-relevant flow is document *content* egress to a third-party provider — ordinary personal data under Art. 6/7, governed by Principle XI's per-provider, revocable consent, which is **once per provider**, not per session (Art. 7(3) makes it revocable; EDPB's best practice is refresh "at appropriate intervals", not per interaction); (iii) if a future feature ever captures user voice (e.g., voice cloning), explicit Art. 9(2)(a) consent becomes mandatory and the design must change — recorded here as a trigger, not a v1 concern. — [source: analysis over the above primary sources] (confidence: high)

## C. CCPA/CPRA (California)

- **§1798.140(c)**: "biometric information" includes "voice recordings, **from which an identifier template, such as ... a voiceprint, can be extracted**". **§1798.140(ae)(2)**: sensitive personal information includes "the processing of biometric information **for the purpose of uniquely identifying a consumer**". No user voice recordings exist in this product → neither provision is triggered by the v1 path. — [source: leginfo.legislature.ca.gov Civ. Code §1798.140; CPPA regs §7001(xx)/(bbb)] (confidence: high)
- **CPRA's sensitive-PI mechanism is opt-out, not per-session opt-in**: notice + "Limit the Use of My Sensitive Personal Information" link / opt-out preference signal (§1798.121, §1798.135, regs §§7014/7027). California imposes *no* per-session consent-prompt obligation anywhere in the statute. — [source: IAPP analysis 2023-11-13; CPPA regulations PDF] (confidence: high)
- Document *contents* sent to a cloud provider may fall within "contents of a consumer's mail, email, and text messages" (§1798.140(ae)(1)(E)) for the receiving business's analysis — again governed by notice/opt-out mechanics, and best handled by the product's own stricter per-provider consent gate (Principle XI) before any egress is attempted. — [source: §1798.140(ae)(1)(E)] (confidence: medium — application to TTS egress is a conservative reading, not settled case law)

## D. Driving-context interruption minimization (NHTSA + Android)

- **NHTSA Visual-Manual Driver Distraction Guidelines for In-Vehicle Electronic Devices, Phase 1** (78 FR 24816, 26 Apr 2013; clarified 79 FR, 16 Sep 2014) — voluntary guidelines, the US design authority for in-vehicle secondary tasks:
  - Acceptance criteria: **mean glance ≤2.0 s; ≤15% of glances >2.0 s; cumulative eyes-off-road ≤12 s** (occlusion method: series of 1.5 s glances, cumulative ≤12 s). Tasks failing the criteria "should be made inaccessible for performance by the driver while driving" — i.e., **deferral is the prescribed remedy**.
  - Design principles: driver's eyes usually on the road; one hand on the wheel; distraction no greater than the baseline task (manual radio tuning); **any task interruptible at any time**; **the driver, not the system, controls the pace**; displays easy to see.
  - Research basis: glances >2 s out of a 6-second window substantially increase crash/near-crash risk (Klauer et al. 2006, 100-Car Naturalistic Driving Study, as cited in the guidelines).
  - "Per se" lock-outs already cover texting, video, browsing while driving. Phase 2 (portable/aftermarket) and Phase 3 (auditory-vocal) were announced; Phase 1 is the operative text. — [source: federalregister.gov 2013-09883, 2014-21991; nhtsa.gov guidelines document] (confidence: high)
- **Android Car app quality guidelines** (developer.android.com/docs/quality-guidelines/car-app-quality, current Oct 2026) — enforced via Google Play review:
  - **DD-2** (Video/Games/Browsers): "While driving, the app must not be launchable and the app's UI must not be visible. The app's audio must stop when the user starts driving and cannot be unpaused while driving." (Exception DD-4: video apps may continue audio on capable devices.) **DD-3**: not launchable/usable while driving, no audio. **DD-1**: navigation audio channel reserved for navigation.
  - **Directly applicable Media criteria**: **MA-1** (no autoplay without user action), **VC-1** (Gemini/Assistant voice commands), **DR-1** (buttons respond ≤2 s), **DR-2** (launch ≤10 s), **DR-3** (content loads ≤10 s), **IN-1** (notifications only when relevant to the driver's needs), and — the closest codification of deferred consent — **VI-1**: "If the user must go to the phone screen — for example, to act on a permission request — then the app must display a message instructing the user to only look at their phone screen **when it's safe to do so**."
  - **Design for Driving principles**: driver reads content and understands system state **within 2 seconds**; system responds within 0.25 s; tasks complete in **≤3 taps**; glanceable text 1–3 lines / ≤120 chars; one-hand gestures; notifications suppressed during maneuvers.
  - **Android Automotive driver-distraction framework** (AOSP): driving state "moving" → `fully_restricted` UX restrictions; only manifest-tagged "Distraction Optimized" activities may run. Notably, the platform enforces *declarations*, not behavior — the same governance shape as this project's deterministic gates (Principle V). — [source: developer.android.com car-app-quality; source.android.com/docs/automotive/driver_distraction; docs.partner.android.com/drivingux design principles] (confidence: high)
- **Precision note on the DD-2 citation**: DD-2 belongs to the Video/Games/Browsers tiers, not Media; and the constitution records Android Auto as out of scope for v1 (no browsing-capable `MediaLibraryService`, no TTS category). DD-2's evidentiary value here is as the *strictest published statement* of the ecosystem's driving-interruption rule; the criteria that would bind a Media-category app (MA-1, VC-1, DR-1, IN-1, VI-1) all point the same direction: **no required attention-requiring interaction while moving**. — [source: car-app-quality tier tables; `constitution.md` Tauri Platform Contract, "Android Auto is out of scope"] (confidence: high)
- **Constitution alignment**: ADR-001 deliberately ducks ~20% and *continues* rather than pausing (continuity over interruption); the notification carries title + section + progress only; every driving control is lock-screen reachable; SC-009 bounds play-to-audio at ≤3 s. A per-session consent prompt violates each of these: it is a visual-manual secondary task mid-drive, it blocks play-to-audio, and it hands pace control to the system — the three things NHTSA's principles and Principle VIII forbid. — [source: `constitution.md` v1.3.0 Sync Impact Report + Driving-Safety Requirements] (confidence: high)

## E. Offline-first synthesis design (Principle IX alignment)

- **Constitution Principle IX**: the engine MUST produce audible speech with no network; `sherpa-onnx` + Piper pt-BR (CC0 voices) is the offline default; "Cloud synthesis is an enhancement layered on top, never the sole path." Rationale: tunnels, rural roads, parking structures. — [source: `constitution.md` §IX, §Engine Registry] (confidence: high — internal governance)
- **GDPR Art. 25 (data protection by design and by default) + Art. 5(1)(c) (data minimisation)**: a zero-egress default is the compliant default; the consent gate belongs exactly where egress begins (the cloud tier), acquired once, per provider, revocable. EDPB Guidelines 02/2021 explicitly recognize edge/local processing as the privacy-favorable paradigm for voice services. — [source: GDPR Art. 25, Art. 5(1)(c); EDPB 02/2021 §2.1] (confidence: high)
- **Android's moving-state model** (fully restricted UX when moving; media apps must not auto-play through car speakers — CarConnection API, `METADATA_KEY_IS_ADVERTISEMENT`) means an app whose required path needs network *plus a fresh prompt* cannot serve the driving state at all. Offline-first is therefore not only a privacy choice but the only state-consistent design. — [source: AOSP Car UX Restrictions; developer.android.com/training/cars/media/distraction-safeguards] (confidence: high)

## F. Application to C3 — the three sub-questions

1. **Defer cloud TTS + consent acquisition to post-v1? — Yes, strongly supported.** No legal instrument requires per-session consent (B, C); per-session prompting is an anti-pattern under the consent-fatigue literature and under GDPR's own "not unnecessarily disruptive" clause (A, B); driving-safety guidance makes mid-drive prompting a violation (D); offline-first is the compliant default (E). The consent *surface* (acquisition UI, storage key, per-provider writer) is a post-v1 feature to be built with the first cloud adapter, acquired in a parked/first-run context.
2. **Rely on the verified on-device engine for the demonstration? — Yes.** On-device synthesis collects no user voice → no Art. 9/CPRA biometric trigger → **no consent surface is needed for on-device synthesis at all** (B, C). This is the strongest possible v1 position: the demonstrated path is the one with the *least* regulatory surface, not a path with consent deferred.
3. **`test_double` for SC-044? — Supported as a verification instrument; the closure verdict stays a governance call.** The evidence supports `test_double` as a legitimate way to exercise the fallback *order* with real audio without introducing a consent surface, cloud dependency, or DPA burden in v1. However, internal rules (`001/spec.md:89`, `002/spec.md:89`) make `test_double` inadmissible once a real adapter is registered, and the 2026-10-06 batch confirmation already resolved C3 as a **split**: consent deferred, SC-044 stays `DEFERRED`. This research does not argue for reopening that verdict; if the parent session does revisit it, the admissibility rule must be amended deliberately (Principle VII coordination), not by research fiat.

## G. Counter-evidence, limits, and change-triggers

- **Stricter reading exists**: EDPB 02/2021 §2.1 says voice data "is inherently biometric personal data". Mitigation: Art. 4(14)'s unique-identification threshold (Recital 51's analogous rule), EBF's and Nautsch et al.'s analyses, and the fact that **no user voice is captured at all**. If a future feature records or clones the user's voice, explicit Art. 9(2)(a) consent is mandatory and per-session prompts would still be the wrong mechanism (a deliberate parked-context grant is).
- **EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689), Art. 50** (applicable since 2 Aug 2026): providers of systems generating synthetic audio must mark outputs in a machine-readable format; deployers must disclose deepfakes (Art. 3(60) three-part test: resembles existing persons/places/events *and* falsely appears authentic). Reading the user's own documents aloud with licensed voices is not a deepfake, so no per-session disclosure is required; voice-cloning features would trigger disclosure — another reason to defer cloud/clone features to a deliberate decision. — [source: eur-lex.europa.eu OJ L 2024/1689, Art. 50; digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu AI Act transparency guidance] (confidence: high)
- **Population-transfer limit**: consent-fatigue magnitudes come from web-banner populations; they do not transfer 1:1 to automotive HMI. The *mechanism* (habituated dismissal) and NHTSA/Android's *quantitative* bounds are population-independent. — (confidence: medium)
- **NHTSA Phase 1 is voluntary**, not an FMVSS — but it is the standard Android's Car quality tiers enforce through Play review, and it is the recognized design authority. — (confidence: high)
- **EDPB best practice**: refresh consent "at appropriate intervals" — this supports a periodic re-ask in settings, not per-session prompting. — [source: EDPB Guidelines 05/2020] (confidence: high)
