# Clarification E2 — Motion accessibility on the restyled driving surface

> **Feature:** `specs/005-adopt-design-sona` (US3/US4 restyle; clarification C5 already pinned the indeterminate bar to a solid sliding pill `--sona-voice` over `--sona-boundary`, 1.5 s loop; D2 pinned the 3 s pulsing yellow dot `#FFD400` and the permanent red `#FF4D63` failure pulse; D5 pinned the TalkBack semantic channel)
> **Question:** Does the restyle's motion budget (perpetual indeterminate slide; 3 s bounded yellow pulse; possibly permanent red pulse; 0.2 s banner slide-in) trigger WCAG 2.1/2.2 SC 2.2.2 "Pause, Stop, Hide" and/or SC 2.3.3 "Animation from Interactions" (AAA) on a driving surface whose clickable inventory is deliberately closed, and what is the sanctioned way to honor OS-level reduced-motion without breaking the closed-hex census? Options: (A) bind 2.2.2-compliant behavior now (freeze under `prefers-reduced-motion` only); (B) add a user-visible pause control (likely rejected for the closed inventory); (C) scope a parametric motion budget (only bounded motions allowed on the driving surface); (D) record an accepted a11y gap analogous to D5's rejected option.
> **Scope:** research note; not a spec amendment. Informs a clarification decision only.
> **Method (2026-10-24):** W3C Understanding 2.2.2 (WCAG 2.1 + 2.2, including Note 4 on preload/essential) and 2.3.3 (AAA); WCAG Conformance Requirement 5 / conformant-alternate-version Note 7; technique C39/SCR40 (`prefers-reduced-motion`); caniuse/chromium-status for Android WebView support; driving-simulator and attention-capture literature (peripheral motion, exogenous orienting, IVIS animation); photosensitive-epilepsy thresholds (Epilepsy Foundation 2005/2024; photosensitive 3–60 Hz, peak 10–25/15–20 Hz); code audit of `src-tauri/src/status_view.html` (indeterminate keyframes, yellow/red CSS, banner slide-in). All URLs below are primary or peer-reviewed where cited.

## 1. What SC 2.2.2 (Level A) literally requires

Normative text (WCAG 2.1/2.2, identical): "For moving, blinking, scrolling, or auto-updating information, all of the following are true: **Moving, blinking, scrolling** — for any moving, blinking or scrolling information that (1) starts automatically, (2) lasts more than five seconds, and (3) is presented in parallel with other content, there is a mechanism for the user to pause, stop, or hide it unless the movement, blinking, or scrolling is part of an activity where it is essential; and **Auto-updating** — for any auto-updating information that (1) starts automatically and (2) is presented in parallel with other content, there is a mechanism for the user to pause, stop, or hide it or to control the frequency of the update unless the auto-updating is part of an activity where it is essential." (https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG21/ ; Understanding: https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/pause-stop-hide.html and https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/pause-stop-hide.html)

Key defined terms from the Understanding page:
- **"starts automatically"** "broadly refers to animations/updates that are not the direct result of a user's intentional activation, for example, selecting a link or button." The indeterminate slide starts on state transition (buffering/indeterminate), not on a tap — so condition (1) is met.
- **"Moving, blinking and scrolling refers to content in which the visible content conveys a sense of motion."** A sliding pill conveys motion. Condition (2) is met whenever the indeterminate state persists >5 s (the prompt says it can persist minutes).
- **"presented in parallel with other content"** — the bar coexists with title/section/transport. Condition (3) met.
- **Mechanism requirement:** "For a mechanism to be considered 'a mechanism for the user to pause,' it must provide the user with a means to pause that does not tie up the user or the focus so that the page cannot be used… Having an animation stop only so long as a user has focus on it (where it restarts as soon as the user moves the focus away) would not be considered a 'mechanism for the user to pause'." So a focus-gated freeze does NOT count.
- **Note 4 (the escape hatch for progress):** "An animation that occurs as part of a preload phase or similar situation can be considered essential if interaction cannot occur during that phase for all users and if not indicating progress could confuse users or cause them to think that content was frozen or broken." This is the only WCAG text that speaks directly to progress indicators.
- **Essential** is defined (in 2.3.3's key terms, reused): "if removed, would fundamentally change the information or functionality of the content, and information and functionality cannot be achieved in another way that would conform."
- **Note 2 (non-interference):** since any content that fails 2.2.2 can interfere with use of the whole page, all content must meet it.

**Applied to the four motions:**

| Motion | Auto-start? | >5 s? | Parallel? | Verdict under 2.2.2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Indeterminate sliding pill (1.5 s loop, minutes-long) | yes (state-driven) | yes (can persist minutes) | yes | **Triggers** unless essential or mechanism provided |
| 3 s yellow pulse (warning TTL) | yes | no (bounded 3 s < 5 s) | yes | **Escapes** (G11 "content that blinks for less than 5 seconds") |
| Permanent red failure pulse (unspecified duration) | yes | possibly (if >5 s) | yes | **Triggers if it persists >5 s** |
| 0.2 s banner slide-in (one-shot) | yes | no (<5 s) | yes | **Escapes** |

So the only 2.2.2 exposure is the **perpetual indeterminate slide** and any **red pulse that outlives 5 s**.

## 2. Is the indeterminate slide "essential"? (the contested point)

Note 4 is the only WCAG text that contemplates progress animation as essential, and it is deliberately narrow: the animation must be (a) part of a preload/similar phase, (b) interaction cannot occur during that phase for all users, and (c) not indicating progress "could confuse users or cause them to think that content was frozen or broken." The indeterminate state is a buffering-class state, which is plausibly a "similar situation" to preload. But two of Note 4's three prongs are weak here:

- **(b) interaction cannot occur for all users** — false in this app. During buffering the user can still pause, skip, and change rate from the notification/lock screen; playback controls remain reachable. The phase is not interaction-locked.
- **(c) not indicating progress could confuse** — true, but only weakly. A static indeterminate track (no motion) is ambiguous ("broken vs. loading"), which is exactly the confusion Note 4 guards against. However, the motion is not the *only* channel: the reason line, the earcon, and the TalkBack status region (D5) all announce buffering. The visual motion is redundant with the audio/semantic channels, which weakens the "essential" claim under WCAG's own definition ("information and functionality cannot be achieved in another way that would conform").

**Conclusion on essential:** the indeterminate slide is **not cleanly essential** under Note 4 because interaction is not locked and the same information is available through non-visual channels. Treating it as essential would be a defensible-but-fragile position that an auditor could challenge, and it would leave no escape for users who find the motion harmful. **It should not be asserted as essential in the spec.** The honest reading is: **the perpetual slide triggers 2.2.2** (YES, with the Note 4 analysis above), and the project must either (i) provide a mechanism, or (ii) not run it continuously, or (iii) gate it on the OS reduced-motion preference as a conformant alternative.

## 3. SC 2.3.3 "Animation from Interactions" (AAA) and vestibular guidance

SC 2.3.3 (Level AAA): "Motion animation triggered by interaction can be disabled, unless the animation is essential to the functionality or the information being conveyed." (https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/animation-from-interactions.html)

- **Scope mismatch:** 2.3.3 governs animation *triggered by interaction* (user-initiated). The indeterminate slide, the red pulse, and the warning pulse are *state-driven* (auto-started by the app), so they fall under 2.2.2, not 2.3.3. The 0.2 s banner slide-in is triggered by a state change (reason appears), not a tap — arguably still auto-started. **2.3.3 is therefore not the controlling SC for this surface; 2.2.2 is.** 2.3.3 is relevant only as the WCAG-sanctioned *mechanism* pattern.
- **Sanctioned mechanisms (from the Understanding page):** "Avoid using unnecessary animation. Provide a control for users to turn off non-essential animations from user interaction. Take advantage of the reduce motion feature in the user agent or operating system." And the sufficient techniques C39 (CSS `prefers-reduced-motion`) and SCR40 (JS `matchMedia`). The Deque material (https://dequeuniversity.com/resources/wcag2.1/2.3.3-animations-from-interactions) confirms: "Because browser support for the 'prefers-reduced-motion' media query is incomplete, web designers … can provide a toggle … However …" — i.e., the OS setting is the preferred, increasingly-sufficient route, with an in-page toggle as fallback for incomplete support. Support is no longer incomplete (see §5).
- **Vestibular severity (from the Understanding page):** "The impact of animation on people with vestibular disorders can be quite severe. Triggered reactions include nausea, migraine headaches, and potentially needing bed rest to recover." And the ta11y summary (https://www.ta11y.org/en/learning/web/moving-content/motion-and-animation/): motion animations "can be very triggering for people with vestibular (inner ear) motion disorders and cause dizziness, nausea, and headaches that can last for many hours," and "these same types of movements can be distracting or cause anxiety for some neurodiverse people."
- **Definition of motion animation:** "addition of steps between conditions to create the illusion of movement or to give a sense of a smooth transition." Note: "Motion animation does not include changes of color, blurring, or opacity which do not change the perceived size, shape, or position of the element." — so a pure opacity pulse (alpha 0.2↔1.0) on the yellow dot is technically *not* "motion animation" under WCAG's definition, but it is "blinking" under 2.2.2's broader "moving, blinking, scrolling." The yellow pulse is blinking, bounded at 3 s, so it escapes via G11 regardless.
- **Essential definition (reused):** "if removed, would fundamentally change the information or functionality of the content, and information and functionality cannot be achieved in another way that would conform." This is the bar any "essential" claim must clear.

## 4. Empirical evidence — peripheral/continuous motion on a glance surface in driving

This is the part that makes the normative question matter beyond compliance.

**Attention capture by peripheral motion (foundational):**
- **Treisman & Gelade 1980 (feature-integration theory)** and **Theeuwes 1992 (stimulus-driven attentional capture)**: abrupt onsets and motion in the periphery capture exogenous (reflexive) attention involuntarily. Muller's driving-attention analysis (CMU, http://www.cs.cmu.edu/~bej/driving/literature/Muller1989.html) applies this directly to in-vehicle displays: "A driver can never completely ignore visual onsets in his/her peripheral vision, so long as the onset causes a sufficient change in light energy… interaction designers must take care to avoid visual onsets except when the driver is already viewing the display." This is the canonical statement that continuous peripheral motion in a car display is a safety-relevant attention captor, not merely a nuisance.
- **Franconeri & Simons 2003** (cited in the DDI 2024 presentation, https://ddi2024.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/4-Method-and-models-Gary.pdf): "Abrupt onsets, looming, as well as concurrent changes in luminance contrast and contrast polarity can lead to attention capture." The DDI 2024 study itself measured VDRT reaction time with in-vehicle animations: "Higher RT in timed compared to baseline (i.e., without animation) vDRT" and "Higher RT for continuous compared to baseline vDRT" — i.e., both onset-type and continuous animations measurably slow the driver's detection response, with continuous motion being worse than none. The authors' conclusion: "Presence of animation affects attention behavior… uncritical with regard to driving safety" only because the absolute RT differences were small in their lab; the gaze-frequency finding ("probability of repeated involuntary gazes is connected with animation duration") is the operative result for a glance surface.

**In-vehicle animation simulator studies:**
- **AHFE 2024** (https://doi.org/10.54941/ahfe1005249): N=53 driving-simulator study of 16 non-driving-related animations differing in salience (fade-in time, brightness, color, internal/external movement) in the cluster vs. central infotainment display. "Visual attention was more vulnerable to animations of increased salience and to animations at lower-effort locations" — i.e., animations in the instrument cluster (where this app's StatusView lives) capture more attention than the same animation in the CID. Slow fade-in and reduced brightness mitigated capture; movement did not. This is direct evidence that a continuously sliding pill in the cluster is the highest-capture configuration.
- **Beirut DBA study** (roadside digital billboards, animated vs. static vs. transitioning): animated billboards produced the longest fixation duration, greatest lane deviation, and most delayed reaction times; transitioning billboards were "equally concerning" (more gazes, back-and-forth checking). Conclusion: "both transitioning and animated DBAs contribute to significant cognitive distraction." The animated case kept attention locked on the display "to see what happens next" — the exact behavior a looping progress pill invites.
- **HUD graphic-warning study** (https://doi.org/10.1177/03611981221134628): animation reduced visual detection time by 25.64% (a benefit for *warning* content), but the benefit did not extend to brake reaction time when combined with borders. Implication: animation is justified only when it carries *safety-critical, time-urgent* information; a progress indicator is not.

**Motion sickness / vestibular:**
- WCAG 2.3.3 Understanding (above) documents vestibular reactions up to bed rest. The pressbooks/iwacc source adds: "A similar effect is motion sickness caused by looking out a side window of a moving car" — i.e., the driving context itself already loads the vestibular system, so in-car display motion compounds an already-stressed system. Night driving further raises sensitivity (photosensitive thresholds below).
- **Photosensitive epilepsy / flicker:** Epilepsy Foundation consensus (2005, updated 2024-2025): flashes ≥20 cd/m² at 3–60 Hz (peak risk 10–25 Hz, especially 15–20 Hz) occupying ≥10% of the visual field are hazardous; red-to-color transitions are especially provocative; patterns with >5 light-dark stripe pairs that oscillate/flash/reverse contrast are hazardous (https://www.epilepsy.com/... ; PMC11995705). The app's 1.5 s loop = 0.67 Hz, well below the 3 Hz photosensitive floor, and the slide is a single solid pill (not a striped pattern), so it is **not** a photosensitive-seizure trigger. This must be stated so the concern is not inflated: the harm here is attention capture and vestibular discomfort, not seizure induction. (The 1.5 s period is also below the WCAG 2.3.1 three-flashes-in-1-s threshold by construction.)

**Peripheral-cue literature (countervailing, bounded):**
- Where et al. (peripheral cues for out-of-view objects, https://uwe-gruenefeld.de/pub/wheretolook-paper.pdf): animated peripheral cues shifted attention in 0.86 s vs. 1.10 s for non-animated — i.e., motion is *faster* at capturing attention. This is the same property that makes it dangerous on a glance surface: it captures attention that should stay on the road. The study's own conclusion was that animated cues are for *critical, time-urgent* relocation of attention, not for status display.

**Bottom line of the empirical record:** continuous peripheral motion on an in-vehicle glance surface (a) involuntarily captures attention (Theeuwes/Muller), (b) increases detection-response time in simulators (DDI 2024), (c) is the most attention-capturing configuration in the cluster position (AHFE 2024), (d) compounds an already-loaded vestibular system in a moving vehicle, and (e) is not a seizure trigger at 0.67 Hz but is a distraction/vestibular trigger. The 3 s bounded pulse and the 0.2 s one-shot are short enough to be sub-threshold for 2.2.2 and for attention-capture habituation; the perpetual slide is the one that matters.

## 5. OS-level accommodation — `prefers-reduced-motion` in Android WebView

- **Chromium/Chrome:** the `prefers-reduced-motion` media query shipped in **Chrome 74** (beta 2019-03-22, stable Android 2019-04-25), per the Chromium blog (https://blog.chromium.org/2019/03/chrome-74-beta-reducing-unwanted-motion.html) and the blink-dev intent-to-ship (https://groups.google.com/a/chromium.org/g/blink-dev/c/NZ3c9d4ivA8). The underlying OS toggle on Android is "Remove animations" in Accessibility settings (Android 9 Pie+), which Chrome 74 honors. The intent doc explicitly confirms Android + Android WebView coverage: "Windows, Mac, and Android are expected to be covered in the initial implementation."
- **WebView:** the media query is available in Android WebView since the Chromium 74-based WebView (WebView switched to Chromium in Chrome 37; features introduced during/after Chrome 37 carry the same version number — https://github.com/mdn/browser-compat-data/blob/main/docs/matching-browser-releases/README.md). caniwebview.com (https://caniwebview.com/features/web-feature-prefers-reduced-motion/) lists the feature as "Widely available" across Android WebView; caniuse reports "Chrome for Android 74+" and "Android Browser 156+" (the latter is a caniuse data artifact, not a real floor — the operative floor for this app's WebView is Chrome 74). Global support is 97.22% (caniuse, 2026).
- **OS toggle location:** Android → Settings → Accessibility → "Remove animations" (Android 9+). The app does not need to provide its own toggle; the OS setting is accessibility-supported and is the sanctioned mechanism (WCAG C39/SCR40: "Take advantage of the reduce motion feature in the user agent or operating system").
- **Sanctioned pattern vs. "weakening exposure":** The question is whether gating motion on `prefers-reduced-motion` (keeping the painted palette identical, census-clean) weakens WCAG exposure or is the sanctioned pattern. **It is the sanctioned pattern, and it does not weaken exposure — it is the exposure's remedy.** Three supports:
  1. **WCAG 2.3.3 Understanding** explicitly lists "Take advantage of the reduce motion feature in the user agent or operating system" as a solution, with C39/SCR40 as sufficient techniques.
  2. **WCAG Conformance Requirement / conformant-alternate-version Note 7:** "Setting user preferences within the content to produce a conforming version is an acceptable mechanism for reaching another version as long as the method used to set the preferences is accessibility supported." The OS toggle is an accessibility-supported preference mechanism; honoring it produces the conforming (motionless) version. This is the canonical WCAG allowance for exactly this situation.
  3. **The "motion-only media query keeps the painted palette identical" property is a feature, not a loophole:** the census (SC-001) audits hex values on the surface files; the motion lives in CSS `@keyframes` and `animation` properties, not in color tokens. Freezing the animation under `prefers-reduced-motion: reduce` changes zero painted pixels, so the closed-hex audit, the contrast audit, and the element inventory are all unaffected. The motion budget becomes a *separate* auditable dimension (keyframes present + gated), not a color-governance problem. This is precisely why the "color-only restyle" framing of feature 005 survives: motion is orthogonal to the color census.
- **Caveat:** `prefers-reduced-motion` is a *preference*, not a *guarantee*. It only helps users who (a) know the setting exists, (b) have enabled it. WCAG 2.3.3 (AAA) accepts this as sufficient; WCAG 2.2.2 (Level A) historically expects an *in-page* mechanism (G186) for auto-started >5 s motion. The gap between "OS setting honored" and "in-page pause control" is the one this clarification must decide.

## 6. The project's own posture (from `DESIGN/accessibility.md`)

- `DESIGN/accessibility.md` §1: "O projeto não define WCAG nível, TalkBack, contentDescription, focus-order, touch-target mínimo de 48 dp, ou screen-reader announcements. O único contrato de acessibilidade é explicitamente documentado: FR-018a / SC-008 (operable locked, by feel, with its effect confirmed audibly)." And: "Nenhum requisito de acessibilidade visual está adicionado sem emenda de spec."
- `DESIGN/accessibility.md` §6 (deliberate non-requirements): "Não há requisito de redução de movimento (exceto prefers-reduced-motion para o ponto amarelo pulsante — ele cessa; não há outra animação)." — i.e., the project already committed to `prefers-reduced-motion` for the yellow pulse and asserted "no other animation." Feature 005's restyle **broke that assertion**: it introduced a perpetual sliding pill, a red pulse, and a banner slide-in. The design doc's "no other animation" claim is now stale and must be amended by this feature (this note is that amendment's evidence).
- The closed element inventory (B4) and the value-blind ladder (FR-012/US3) mean adding a pause control to the indeterminate bar would be a new interactive element on the driving surface — exactly the kind of addition the inventory was closed to prevent, and one that would need an earcon, a touch target, a blind-test entry, and a TalkBack role (D5). The cost is high and the benefit (a redundant control on top of the OS setting + audio channels) is low.

## 7. Honest gaps and limits

- I did not retrieve a primary study measuring *vestibular* (not attentional) effects of in-car display motion specifically; the vestibular claim rests on WCAG 2.3.3's documented severity and the "side window of a moving car" analogy, plus the compounding argument (driving already loads the vestibular system). The attention-capture claim is well-supported by primary simulator studies; the vestibular claim is standards-supported but not empirically quantified for this exact configuration.
- I did not find a primary source for whether TalkBack/Android announces CSS animations or honors `prefers-reduced-motion` for *web content inside a WebView* beyond the Chromium-level media query support; the assumption is that the WebView inherits the OS setting, which Chrome 74's intent doc confirms for Android but not with an explicit WebView test. The device trial (SC-007) should include a reduced-motion check.
- The "essential" analysis in §2 is an interpretation; WCAG has no progress-bar-specific ruling beyond Note 4. An auditor could read Note 4 more permissively (buffering ≈ preload). The spec should record the position taken so it is reviewable.
- Photosensitive-seizure risk is not a concern at 0.67 Hz (below the 3 Hz floor), but the red pulse's color transition to/from saturated red is flagged by the Epilepsy Foundation consensus as especially provocative *if* it flashes ≥3 Hz. The pulse must stay below 3 Hz (e.g., 1.2 s period or slower) and ideally be a single non-repeating pulse, not a loop.

## Implication for the spec

**Recommendation: Option A + a documented, bounded carve-out — not B, not a silent gap.** Concretely:

1. **Bind 2.2.2-compliant behavior now, via the OS setting, not an in-page control (Option A).** Add to FR-001/US3/SC-001 a motion clause: every motion on the driving surface (indeterminate slide, red failure pulse, banner slide-in) MUST be gated on `@media (prefers-reduced-motion: no-preference)` (CSS) / `window.matchMedia('(prefers-reduced-motion: reduce)')` (JS, SCR40), and MUST be frozen (animation: none; transform: none; instant state) when the preference is `reduce`. Under reduced motion, the indeterminate bar renders as a static solid pill at a fixed position (or as a static indeterminate track) — the TalkBack status region (D5) and the reason line carry the "in progress" semantics. This is the WCAG-sanctioned mechanism (C39/SCR40 + conformant-alternate-version Note 7) and it costs zero painted pixels, so SC-001's color census is untouched.

2. **Do NOT add an in-page pause control (reject Option B).** The closed element inventory (B4) and the project's own accessibility.md §6 (no motion-reduction requirement beyond the yellow pulse) make a pause control a high-cost, low-value addition: it would need an earcon, a 48 dp target, a blind-test entry, and a TalkBack role, and it would duplicate the OS setting that 97.22% of browsers honor and that WCAG C39/SCR40 already sanction. The inventory stays closed. Record this rejection explicitly so the decision is reviewable (mirroring D5's "rejected option" traceability).

3. **Scope a parametric motion budget (Option C, partially):** only *bounded* motions are permitted to run unconditionally on the driving surface; perpetual motions are permitted only under `prefers-reduced-motion: no-preference`. Specifically:
   - **Bounded, allowed unconditionally:** the 3 s yellow pulse (already <5 s, escapes 2.2.2 via G11; already ceases under reduced motion per design doc) and the 0.2 s banner slide-in (one-shot, <5 s). These are short enough to be sub-threshold for both 2.2.2 and attention-capture habituation.
   - **Perpetual, gated:** the indeterminate slide and any red failure pulse. The red pulse MUST additionally be bounded in duration (recommend: single pulse or ≤5 s total, then settle to a static red state) — an unbounded red pulse would fail 2.2.2 outright (no essential claim available for a terminal failure state) and would keep attention locked on a failure the user cannot act on. If a longer "still failing" indication is needed, it must be static (color + position + earcon), not animated.
   - The 1.5 s loop period (0.67 Hz) stays — it is below the 3 Hz photosensitive floor, so seizure risk is not a concern; the concern is attention/vestibular, which the reduced-motion gate addresses.

4. **Record the accepted a11y gap (Option D, in a narrow form):** the project does NOT provide an in-page pause/stop/hide mechanism for the indeterminate slide; it relies on the OS reduced-motion setting + the non-visual channels (earcon, reason line, TalkBack status region). This is a documented, reviewable gap analogous to D5's "host audit is a regression guard, not proof" — the device trial (SC-007) must include a reduced-motion leg (TalkBack + "Remove animations" enabled → verify the slide freezes and the buffering state is still announced). Do NOT record a blanket "a11y gap" that waives 2.2.2; record the specific, scoped reliance on the OS setting plus the non-visual channels.

5. **Amend `DESIGN/accessibility.md` §6**, which currently asserts "Não há requisito de redução de movimento (exceto prefers-reduced-motion para o ponto amarelo pulsante — ele cessa; não há outra animação)." Feature 005 introduced three new motions, so that sentence is now false. Replace with: reduced motion is honored for all four motions (yellow pulse, indeterminate slide, red pulse, banner slide-in); the indeterminate slide and red pulse are gated on `prefers-reduced-motion`; the yellow pulse and banner slide-in are bounded and also cease under reduced motion. This is the same amendment pattern as D5's "semantics is NOT visual, so that note does not block it" — motion reduction IS visual, so the note must be amended, not bypassed.

6. **Add to SC-001/SC-007 (host + device):** (host) a deterministic check that every `@keyframes`/`animation` in the touched surface files is either (a) bounded to <5 s and not looping, or (b) wrapped in `@media (prefers-reduced-motion: no-preference)`; an unguarded infinite/long animation fails. (device) SC-007's TalkBack trial gains a reduced-motion leg: with "Remove animations" on, verify (10/10) that the indeterminate slide freezes, the red pulse does not loop, and the buffering/failure state is still announced via the status region + reason line.

7. **Do not inflate into a compliance program.** Per D5's EAA analysis (personal maintainer, microenterprise exemption, app not a covered product/service), there is no legal pressure. The decision is dogfood quality + the project's own Driving-First (Principle VIII) safety bar, which is the stronger of the two arguments: the empirical record (§4) shows continuous peripheral motion in the cluster is the highest-attention-capture configuration, so gating it on the OS setting is a safety improvement even for users who never touch the setting (the default still runs — but at least the OS-level opt-out exists, which is more than the current "no motion reduction except the pulse" posture provides). If the maintainer wants to go further, the next step is a *default-off* perpetual motion (run the slide only for the first 5 s, then freeze to static) — that is the strictest reading of 2.2.2 without an in-page control, and it is offered here as a stronger alternative worth weighing, not as the recommendation, because a 5 s cap on a buffering indicator that may last minutes would make "still buffering" indistinguishable from "frozen" — exactly the confusion Note 4 warns against. The recommended middle path (gate on OS setting + bounded red pulse) preserves progress legibility for the common case while giving vestibular-sensitive users a real off switch.

### Open items for the maintainer

- Whether the indeterminate slide should also be capped at 5 s by default (stronger 2.2.2 reading) or only gated on the OS setting (recommended). The trade-off is progress-legibility vs. compliance-strictness; the device trial should include both configurations if budget allows.
- Whether the red failure pulse should be a single non-repeating pulse (recommended) or a short loop; either way it must not outlive 5 s unguarded.
- Whether to add a host-side `prefers-reduced-motion` smoke test in the device leg (recommended, low cost).
- Whether `DESIGN/accessibility.md` §6 is amended in this feature (recommended) or deferred; deferral leaves a documented contradiction in the repo.
